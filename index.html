Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring · Machine Learning Capstone

Which pages are about to lose their search visibility — and what should you do about each one first?

A client-grouped, leakage-checked classifier for content decline risk, built on 79 million rows of real production search data — with an honest accounting of what it can and can't tell you.

Rows analyzed 78,835,655 Clients 104 Method Random Forest, grouped split Data source FlyRank ML Internship warehouse

01 · Abstract

Abstract

Content teams can't manually review every page every week, so this study asks a narrow, practical question: given a portfolio of pages, which ones are about to lose search impressions, and what should a human do first? Using FlyRank's real 79-million-row production search warehouse, I built an 8-feature Random Forest classifier on 102,203 content items, validated with a client-grouped split so the reported performance reflects generalization to unseen clients rather than memorized ones. The model beat both a majority-class and a transparent rule-based baseline on that honest split (ROC-AUC 0.63, balanced accuracy 0.59) — modest, but real, and I explicitly quantified the cost of skipping proper grouping: a naive random split overstated the same model's F1 by 0.156. The output is a ranked, reason-coded action queue — not a black box, and not a guarantee about any single page.

02 · Introduction

The problem this supports

FlyRank's own March 2026 research paper names its top playbook priority in one line: refresh mature pages before they decay. That's an aggregate, portfolio-level finding — content peaks around 61–90 days and falls off a cliff after roughly nine months. It tells a team that refresh works. It doesn't tell a specific team, looking at their specific 4,000-page portfolio on a Monday morning, which twenty pages to open first.

That's the gap this work tries to close: not a replacement for the aggregate finding, but a bottom-up, page-level triage layer built on top of it — a classifier a content team could actually run against their own backlog, with a stated reason for every page it flags.

What this is not A claim about why Google ranks pages the way it does, or a guarantee about any single page's future. It's a decision-support triage signal — Sections 4–6 show exactly how strong that signal is, and where it stops being trustworthy.

04 · Methodology

Methodology

Label

is_declining = 1 if impressions_last30 < 0.8 × impressions_prev30 — a >20% month-over-month drop. Relative to each page's own history, not a portfolio average, since baseline traffic varies enormously by page.

Features

Eight signals, every one sourced from the prev-30 window or the query-mix table — never the outcome window: prior impressions, clicks, average position, click-through rate, distinct queries ranked for, rare-impression share, anonymized-impression share, and top-query concentration.

Baselines — two, not one

A majority-class rule (always predict the more common label) and a transparent hand-rule: flag a page if its average position sits in the worst quartile.

Validation design

Client-grouped split (GroupShuffleSplit on client ID): 36 training clients, 13 held-out test clients, zero overlap, confirmed by assertion. A random row-level split was tested and rejected — it let the model partly learn which client a row belonged to, not a pattern that generalizes. The cost of that mistake is measured directly in Section 5.

Leakage checks

Three checks, run against the final feature set: (1) confirmed the label-defining column never appears among the features; (2) a single-feature AUC sweep — nothing exceeded 0.616, well under the 0.90 red-flag line; (3) an explicit date-math check confirming the query-mix table's 90-day window partially overlaps the 30-day outcome window.

Named limitation, not hidden Because of that overlap, the four query-mix features are treated as directional/descriptive throughout this work, not strictly pre-outcome predictors.

A genuinely surprising result: average position — the single feature FlyRank's own paper found most predictive of its Health Score (43% importance) — carries almost no unique signal here once the other features are already in the model. Where it's wrong

False positive rate 7.8% of the test set; false negative rate 39.9%. Errors skew toward missed declines, not false alarms — worth knowing for how a team weights this in triage.

06 · Limitations

What this work cannot claim

  • Not causalEvery result here is observational. Position, CTR, and query-mix are associated with decline, not proven causes of it.
  • Modest powerROC-AUC of 0.630 is meaningfully better than chance but far from strong. Recall of 0.41 means the model misses roughly 6 in 10 pages that actually go on to decline — a triage aid, not a certainty engine.
  • Client scopeValidated on 49 clients (36 train / 13 test) out of 104 in the warehouse. No claim is made about clients outside this mix of industries and traffic patterns.
  • Window overlapThe four query-mix features have a known partial overlap with the outcome window (Section 4) — treated as directional, not strictly pre-outcome.
  • No external contextThe model has no way to distinguish an organic decline from a seasonal dip, a manual penalty, or a site migration.
  • Fold asymmetryTrain/test base rates differ (58.7% vs. 67.7%) purely because of which clients landed in each fold — an expected consequence of grouping by a relatively small number of clients, not an error.

Human review — required, not optional

No page in this queue should be auto-published, auto-merged, or auto-redirected. Every flag needs a human check: does the drop line up with something the model can't see (seasonality, a tracked algorithm update, a site change)? Is the reason code what a person reading the page would actually conclude? Is the page worth the effort given its traffic volume?

Monitoring triggers

Retrain or re-evaluate if: the portfolio's base decline rate drifts meaningfully from 63.3%; feature medians shift from training distributions; held-out balanced accuracy drops below ~0.585 on a fresh client group; or on a fixed quarterly floor regardless of drift signals.
